The moment I keep coming back to in game development is the first playtest: someone meets a system you know by heart and sees something you never anticipated. Sometimes it is a bug. Sometimes it is a better idea. I like working in that space between the code, the team, and the person playing.

That curiosity has taken me from programming a collapsing desktop full of minigames to leading QA on a Steam release. Lately it has also pushed me toward software that makes creative work easier to understand and improve.

## On my desk right now

- **[PlaytestIQ](https://github.com/suvnset/playtestiq)** — an in-progress playtest telemetry tool. I am building its Next.js/TypeScript foundation and PostgreSQL data model around projects, players, sessions, and events. The goal is to turn questions like *where did players get stuck?* into evidence a small game team can actually use. [See the project notes](https://suvnset.github.io/project.html?project=playtestiq).
- **[Deer Population Monitoring](https://github.com/Martinez474/DeerPopulationMonitoring)** — a team simulation of a Catalina Island deer survey in Webots. My recent work explores drone views, time-of-day effects, deer behavior, and simulated thermal detection. It is a different kind of interactive system, but it asks a familiar question: how do you make the model legible enough to trust? [See the portfolio write-up](https://suvnset.github.io/project.html?project=deer-population-monitoring).

- **[Glorgo's Microplastics Mine](https://suvnset.github.io/project.html?project=glorgos-microplastics-mine)** · QA lead on a nearly 80-person USC Games project [released on Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4246790/Glorgos_Microplastics_Mine/). I organized testing and bug triage, built a Python Discord bot to keep reports visible where the team already talked, and worked with engineering to turn hard-to-reproduce problems into actionable fixes. It taught me that QA is as much about communication as it is about finding defects.
- **[Open Your Browser](https://suvnset.github.io/project.html?project=open-your-browser)** · Unity programmer on a game that turns a student's frantic desktop into the play space. I worked on the main loop, RAM pressure, scene flow, and a cookie rhythm minigame timed to music. Making the chaos feel intentional meant caring about the small transitions as much as the big mechanics.
- **[Neuromancer](https://suvnset.github.io/project.html?project=neuromancer)** · programmer on an Open Alpha cohort game about an evil wizard counseling their defeated minions. I helped plan modular Unity systems and connect menus, scenes, and the game's dialogue and progression flow. It was a lesson in making many contributors' work feel like one game.

## The people part

I have spent a lot of time in game communities as well as game engines: programming with Open Alpha, serving as president of USC's Makers of Entertaining Games Association, designing for esports, and writing and directing [PACN: Pamana](https://suvnset.github.io/project.html?project=pacn-pamana) with Troy Philippines. I also photograph people and places, and I follow competitive games with the kind of attention usually reserved for patch notes.

Those experiences keep me interested in the same thing from different angles: how a group of people makes something together, and how the details of that work reach the person on the other side of the screen. If you are building a game, a tool for creators, or software that needs both technical care and a human point of view, I would love to talk.
